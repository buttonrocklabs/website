> I once spent the better part of a day rebuilding the same screen eleven times. Not because it was broken. Because each version was *almost* right, and the next one was only a prompt away. Round button or square. Copper or stone. Icon left, icon right, no icon. Each change took ninety seconds and felt like progress. By the time I looked up, I had eleven variants of a screen no user had asked me to touch, and the actual feature I sat down to build was exactly where I left it that morning.
>
> Nothing forced me to stop. That was the problem.

There is an old product management line: *if you don't know where you're going, any road will get you there.* It was a warning about wasted effort. In the world of vibe coding it has become something sharper, because now every road is open, fast, and feels productive.

## The seduction

When I wrote that [the prototype is the product](/blog/the-prototype-is-the-product), I meant it. The collapse in the cost of building is real and it is wonderful. You can have an idea at breakfast and be holding a working version of it by lunch.

The seduction is that cheap iteration *feels* like progress. Every prompt produces something. The screen changes, the diff lands, the app reloads. Your brain reads motion as movement. And because each step is nearly free, there is no natural moment where you stop and ask whether this step was worth taking.

That moment used to be built in. It had a price tag attached.

## The hidden cost

When building was expensive, cost did your thinking for you. A six-figure development contract forces a conversation: do we really want this? Is it worth a sprint? Every feature had to survive a budget. The friction was annoying, and it was also a filter.

Vibe coding removes the friction. It does not remove the need for the filter. The cost did not disappear, it just moved somewhere you don't see it: the hours you pour into "what about this, what about that" loops with no destination. You don't get an invoice for those. They just quietly become your week.

Here is the part that took me too long to learn: building cheap things fast in the wrong direction is more wasteful than building expensive things slowly in the right one. Slow and expensive at least made you choose a direction. Fast and cheap lets you accumulate sunk-cost gravity in a direction you never actually picked.

## Why the fundamentals matter more, not less

It is tempting to think that if the agent can build anything, the skill is in the prompting. It isn't. The agent made the building part easy, which means the building part is no longer where the value is.

The value moved to the things the agent cannot do for you: knowing your market, knowing the human you're building for, knowing what success looks like before you ship, and knowing when to stop. None of those are coding skills. All of them are harder to fake now that there is nowhere to hide behind a long build cycle.

The skills that compound in this era are the ones that point you at the right road. The skills that quietly degrade are judgment, taste, and the ability to ship something and walk away instead of tinkering forever. You have to exercise those on purpose now, because nothing about the tools will force you to.

## The discipline

Discipline is just the friction you add back yourself. This is the checklist I actually use:

- **Define done before you start.** Write down what the feature is and what it is not. If you can't say what done looks like, you are not ready to build it, you are ready to wander.
- **Set a budget, in time and in tokens.** Give the work a boundary up front. When you hit it, you stop and decide on purpose, instead of drifting.
- **Ship it and walk away.** The goal is a thing in front of a real user, not a thing that satisfies you. Those are different, and the second one has no end.
- **Resist the one more variant.** The eleventh version of a screen is almost never worth more than the second. Let the market tell you it needs changing.
- **Let the market teach you, not your own taste.** Your taste is an input, not the judge. The user's reaction is the judge.

I am honest enough about my own discipline leaking that I measure it. I track repair loops per feature on my own work, because I needed to see where I was redoing things that were already done. You cannot manage what you refuse to look at, and the "what about" loops are exactly the kind of cost that hides unless you go looking for it.

## The bigger picture

Tokens feel cheap right now. They feel cheap because we still compare them to dev teams, offshore contracts, and protracted sprints. That comparison is fading. As it does, our expectations will reset, and the same spend that feels like a steal today will feel like "where did all my money go" in a year. The discipline you build now is what will keep that day from being a reckoning.

Vibe coding is a tool. A remarkable one. But a tool does not know where you are going, and it will happily take you down any road you point it at, quickly and cheaply and forever. The value was never in the building. It is in the human deciding what is worth building, and having the discipline to stop when it's built.
